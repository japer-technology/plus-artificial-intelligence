# AI Helped. I Take Responsibility.

AI is already in the room.

It is helping us write.

It is helping us think.

It is helping us analyse data, design products, write software, prepare reports, create images, make decisions and solve problems.

Sometimes we admit it.

Sometimes we don’t.

And increasingly, I think we are asking the wrong question.

We keep asking:

**“Did AI make this?”**

I think there is a more important question.

**“Who takes responsibility for it?”**

Because an AI can generate an answer.

But it cannot take responsibility for sending it.

An AI can write a piece of software.

But it cannot take responsibility for deploying it.

It can produce a medical summary, a legal argument, a financial model, a recommendation, a design, an essay or a presentation.

But at some point, somebody decides:

**This is good enough.**

**This is accurate enough.**

**This represents what I mean.**

**I am willing to put my name on this.**

That decision is still profoundly human.

---

For centuries, we have developed ways of indicating responsibility.

A painter signs a painting.

An author puts their name on a book.

An engineer signs off a drawing.

A doctor signs a report.

A company director signs an annual statement.

The signature doesn't mean that person physically performed every task involved.

A chief executive doesn't type every page of an annual report.

An architect doesn't manufacture every brick.

A film director doesn't operate every camera.

Authorship has almost always involved assistance.

What matters is that eventually somebody says:

**I stand behind this.**

AI has complicated that.

Because now the assistance can be extraordinary.

I can ask an artificial intelligence to draft something in seconds that might once have taken me an afternoon.

I can challenge it.

Rewrite it.

Ask it for ten alternatives.

Ask another AI to criticise the first one.

I can use AI to research, analyse, edit, calculate, translate and create.

So where does the AI end and the human begin?

Increasingly, that may be impossible to answer.

And perhaps it isn't even the question that matters.

---

Imagine I give you a report.

At the bottom it says:

**“Created without artificial intelligence.”**

What does that actually tell you?

It tells you something about the process.

It tells you almost nothing about whether the report is correct.

Now imagine another report.

AI was used extensively.

It helped find evidence.

It challenged assumptions.

It reorganised the argument.

It corrected mistakes.

The person responsible examined the output, rejected parts of it, checked the important claims and finally decided to publish it.

Which report should you trust?

You can't answer from the presence or absence of AI.

The important question is still:

**Who evaluated the result?**

And:

**Who is prepared to answer for it?**

---

We are entering a strange period where people sometimes hide the use of AI because they fear that disclosure somehow diminishes their work.

I think that is going to become increasingly absurd.

AI is becoming part of the intellectual infrastructure.

We don't normally disclose:

“I used a spreadsheet.”

“I used spellcheck.”

“I searched the internet.”

“I used a calculator.”

But artificial intelligence is different in one important respect.

It can contribute materially to the thinking and expression of the work.

So I believe there is value in acknowledging that contribution.

But acknowledgement shouldn't become an escape hatch.

We cannot allow:

“The AI did it.”

to become the intellectual equivalent of:

“The dog ate my homework.”

If I choose to use an AI, I remain responsible for that choice.

If I accept its answer, that is my decision.

If I publish its words, that is my decision.

If I deploy its code, that is my decision.

The machine can assist me.

The machine cannot absolve me.

---

A little while ago, I started wondering whether we needed a very simple way of saying that.

Not a certification.

Not a licence.

Not a 20-page AI disclosure statement.

Not a percentage saying:

“37 per cent AI-generated.”

Because I don't think anybody could reliably calculate that anyway.

I wanted something closer to a convention.

Something like a post-nominal.

Three characters.

**+AI**

Placed after a person's name.

**Eric Mourant +AI**

It means:

**AI helped. I take responsibility.**

That's all.

It doesn't mean the work is good.

It doesn't mean the AI was correct.

It doesn't certify expertise.

It doesn't tell you which model was used.

It doesn't claim that the human wrote every word.

In fact, it explicitly says the opposite.

AI materially assisted.

But a human being is putting their name to the result.

---

Something happened almost immediately that made me think the idea might have legs.

I showed it to a colleague I've known for about thirty years.

He is a very conservative man.

Not politically conservative — professionally conservative.

Governance.

Management.

Accountability.

The sort of person who thinks very carefully before putting his name on something.

I explained the idea to him.

And almost immediately, he started using it.

He didn't link it to the website.

He didn't write an explanation.

He didn't advertise it.

He simply put:

**+AI**

after his name.

And I found that fascinating.

Because that is what notation does when it works.

You don't explain “PhD” every time you see it.

You don't explain “©”.

You don't explain an asterisk.

The symbol carries an idea.

And eventually, if a convention becomes understood, the explanation disappears.

---

Now, I have no idea whether +AI will become such a convention.

Maybe it will disappear.

Maybe someone will invent something better.

Maybe ten years from now we'll look back and wonder why we ever thought AI assistance required any special notation at all.

But I think the principle underneath it matters enormously.

We are spending a great deal of time asking how much of our work will be done by machines.

I think we should spend at least as much time deciding what humans remain responsible for.

Because responsibility cannot be automated away simply because production can be.

The more powerful these systems become, the more important that distinction becomes.

An AI may eventually produce an entire engineering design.

Someone must decide whether to build it.

It may produce a diagnosis.

Someone must decide whether to act on it.

It may produce a political argument.

Someone must decide whether to publish it.

It may produce a piece of software affecting millions of people.

Someone must decide whether to deploy it.

Generation is not responsibility.

Capability is not accountability.

And assistance is not authorship.

---

I suspect the future will contain an enormous amount of work that is neither purely human nor purely artificial.

It will simply be work.

Human judgement amplified by machines.

Machines directed, questioned, corrected and ultimately authorised by people.

And perhaps what we need isn't a way to pretend that artificial intelligence wasn't involved.

Perhaps we need the opposite.

A way to say:

Yes.

AI helped.

I'm not hiding that.

I'm also not hiding behind it.

---

This talk was developed with artificial intelligence.

Some of the words you have heard were suggested by a machine.

Some were rejected.

Some were rewritten.

Some ideas were mine.

Some became clearer because an AI challenged me.

But there is one thing the AI could not do.

It could not decide to stand here.

It could not put my name on this idea.

And if anything I have said today is wrong, foolish, provocative or worth arguing about…

the AI will not be answering your questions afterwards.

I will.

My name is:

**Eric Mourant +AI**

AI helped.

**I take responsibility.**
