# +AI: The Possibility Space

## A definitive exploration of identity, declarations, directories, public benefit, funding, and premium services

> **Status:** strategic exploration, not part of the normative `+AI` specification
> **Date:** 2026-09-03
> **Core meaning preserved:** **AI helped. I take responsibility.**
> **Next stage:** the ideas here became the catalogue in [`docs/suggestions/`](../suggestions/), then the plan in [`docs/planning/`](../planning/).

---

## Executive answer

`+AI` can become much more than a multilingual explanation page, but it should
not become a tollbooth around the notation.

The strongest destination is a **public accountability commons** with optional
services around it:

1. Anyone can use `+AI`, understand it, create a personalised link, and make a
   portable declaration without an account or payment.
2. People and organisations can optionally publish stable profiles,
   artifact-specific declarations, and an **AI practice register** describing
   the tools and practices they choose to disclose.
3. An opt-in directory makes those public declarations discoverable without
   pretending that listing is endorsement, certification, or proof that the
   underlying work is correct.
4. Open formats, self-hosting, static exports, verification tools, and
   interoperability keep the public layer portable and resistant to capture.
5. Revenue comes from saving organisations time and operational effort:
   workspaces, integrations, private registers, high-volume APIs, managed
   hosting, training, implementation support, and evidence exports.
6. Donations, grants, memberships, and sponsorships can fund the commons if
   finances and conflicts are transparent and no funder can buy influence over
   the definition, directory, or evidence labels.

The essential rule is:

> **Keep the declaration free. Charge for workflow, scale, support, and
> convenience—never for permission, credibility, or visibility.**

The immediate website gap is also clear. The project needs visible routes to:

- **Why this exists**
- **Who originated it**
- **How to use it**
- **How to help build it**
- **How it is governed**
- **How to support it financially**

Those pages should make Eric Mourant visible as the originator while making an
equally visible promise that the notation is intended to outgrow dependence on
one person or one company.

## 1. Ground truth: what already exists

The project is not starting from an idea alone. It already has an unusually
strong public foundation:

| Asset | Present capability | Strategic value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Canonical meaning | `+AI` means AI materially assisted and a named party accepts responsibility | A simple, memorable social convention |
| Normative specification | Draft v0.1 with 26 sections | A precise definition that resists semantic drift |
| Languages | 40 translated specification files | Global reach and an inclusion advantage |
| Presentations | Approximately 80 themed, single-file pages | Audience-specific expression without changing the meaning |
| Personalisation | `name`, `company`, `lang`, `theme`, `font`, and highlight/accent URL parameters | No-account, author-controlled links |
| Toolkit | URL, QR, plain-text, HTML, Markdown, badge, and comparison tools | An existing adoption funnel |
| Architecture | Static S3/CloudFront site, mirrorable and usable without a server-side application | Low cost, resilience, and privacy |
| Roadmap | Assertions, hashing, registry, checker, certificates, embeds, and standards alignment | A credible route from explanation to trust infrastructure |

The existing URL behaviour is already a prototype for author-controlled
presentation. For example, a link can select a themed file and supply a name,
company, language, font, display mode, and highlight colour. The browser then
substitutes the example names in the specification.

That is useful, but it is important to state what it is **not**:

- It is not proof that the named person created the link.
- It is not proof that the named person controls a domain or mailbox.
- It is not a stored profile.
- It is not an artifact-specific declaration.
- It is not a statement that a named tool was actually used.
- It is not verification, certification, or endorsement.

The missing layer is therefore not more visual variety. It is the human and
institutional layer around the existing work: origin, motivation, participation,
durable declarations, discovery, governance, and sustainability.

### 1.1 Existing constitutional constraints

The best future preserves the principles already present in the
[specification](../../site/SPECIFICATION.md),
[plan](../THE-PLAN.md), and
[core extensions roadmap](../core-extensions.md):

1. The mark remains free to use without payment, registration, permission,
   membership, certification, or an approved AI provider.
2. One normative meaning may have many presentations.
3. A signer chooses the presentation; the project protects the meaning.
4. Static, offline, self-hosted, and mirrorable use remains possible.
5. A specification or declaration cannot silently change underneath an old
   link.
6. A declaration is not a warranty that the work is correct, safe, lawful, or
   professionally adequate.
7. Registration and public listing remain optional.
8. Evidence of control must never be represented as endorsement or merit.

These are not obstacles to a business model. They define a trustworthy one.

### 1.2 A foundational issue to resolve

The repository currently has no top-level licence file. Before inviting broad
contribution, reuse, mirroring, or commercial integration, the project should
make deliberate and legally reviewed decisions about:

- the licence for software;
- the licence for specification and educational content;
- the licence for translations and contributed themes;
- the licence for public registry data;
- permitted use of the project name and visual identity; and
- an irrevocable free-use promise for the `+AI` notation in its canonical
  sense.

Open intent is valuable; explicit permissions are what make it dependable.

## 2. The north star: a commons with services around it

The project should distinguish several objects that are easy to blur together.

| Object | What it answers | Should an account be required? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Mark** | “What does `+AI` mean?” | Never |
| **Explanation link** | “How do I present that meaning to my audience?” | Never |
| **Declaration** | “Who accepts responsibility for this artifact or scope?” | No |
| **Profile** | “Who is this person or organisation, and what have they chosen to publish?” | Optional |
| **AI practice register** | “Which AI capabilities, tools, rules, or review practices do they declare?” | No for portable/self-hosted use; optional for hosting |
| **Directory** | “Which opt-in public profiles or declarations can I discover?” | Never for reading |
| **Evidence** | “What control or technical consistency has actually been checked?” | No for reading; an account may simplify issuance |
| **Certification** | “Has an independent body judged conformance to defined criteria?” | A separate future programme, if ever created |
| **Premium service** | “Which operational burden is the service taking away?” | Usually |

The **declaration** should be the primary unit. A profile is useful context, but
responsibility attaches to a work, communication, policy, or defined scope—not
automatically to every activity of a person forever.

This distinction prevents three damaging outcomes:

- turning `+AI` into a social status or personality label;
- implying that a directory profile makes all of a person's work trustworthy;
  and
- letting a commercial account system become the only way to make the
  declaration.

### 2.1 The public promise

A short public-benefit covenant could state:

> The `+AI` notation, canonical definition, core educational material,
> no-account declaration tools, basic verification, and portable public formats
> will remain available without payment or permission. Optional paid services
> may make them easier to manage at scale, but will not make a declaration more
> legitimate.

That promise should eventually be reinforced through licences, governance, and
entity documents rather than existing only as website copy.

## 3. Put the human story and the invitations on the website

The website currently explains the notation far more thoroughly than it
explains the person, motivation, project, or invitation behind it. That can be
fixed without inserting fundraising into the normative specification.

### 3.1 The minimum public navigation

The canonical page and any future shared engine should offer a quiet,
consistent route to a non-normative project footer or menu containing:

| Page | Purpose | Primary action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Why** | Explain the problem: AI disclosure exists, but human responsibility still needs a name | Understand the idea |
| **About Eric** | Tell the origin story and identify the first person willing to sign it | Meet the originator |
| **Use +AI** | Provide copy, link, email, badge, document, and code examples | Adopt it now |
| **Help build** | List specific contribution needs | Volunteer or propose a pilot |
| **Community** | Show signatories, contributors, case studies, and open discussions | Participate |
| **Governance** | Publish stewardship, decisions, conflicts, licences, and policies | Inspect accountability |
| **Support** | Explain costs, funding, donations, grants, and paid services | Contribute money or resources |
| **Contact** | Provide routes for media, partnerships, safety, privacy, and general questions | Reach the right channel |

The existing subdomain wishlist already anticipates useful names such as
`about`, `community`, `contact`, `help`, `registry`, `support`, and `verify`.
They can be memorable aliases, but the content should also have ordinary,
canonical paths so it does not depend on maintaining a large fleet of
subdomains.

Do not hand-edit approximately 80 archive themes to add this navigation and
create another drift surface. Add it to archive pages only through the shared
`data-text` hooks and the planned conformance linter/generator; until that
exists, keep the canonical and future engine pages as the guaranteed routes.

### 3.2 The founder story

The strongest origin story is simple and already implicit in the project:

> I was using artificial intelligence to help think, write, design, and build.
> Existing disclosure language felt clumsy or defensive. I wanted to
> acknowledge that help without surrendering human responsibility, so I began
> signing AI-assisted work: **Eric Mourant +AI**.

The page should also answer:

- What moment or repeated experience made the problem feel urgent?
- When was the canonical responsibility meaning first written down?
- What did AI materially help create in this project?
- Which decisions did Eric make and accept responsibility for?
- What prior uses or adjacent ideas exist?
- What is being claimed as the contribution, and what is not?
- Why is the work published openly?
- What kind of future stewardship is intended?

The founder should be visible without becoming the gatekeeper. A useful
stewardship statement is:

> Originated by Eric Mourant. Maintained openly by the +AI project. The
> originator should remain part of the history, not become a permanent point of
> permission.

### 3.3 Ask for help precisely

“Help us” is too vague. The contribution page should offer bounded jobs:

- review a translation, especially normative terms;
- test the explanation with people unfamiliar with the project;
- document a real use case;
- run a small institutional pilot;
- improve accessibility or low-bandwidth delivery;
- review privacy, child-safety, trademark, or governance proposals;
- map `+AI` to a professional or publication disclosure rule;
- design a neutral theme or export format;
- build an integration;
- help moderate public directory submissions;
- join a specification or community working group;
- provide infrastructure, fiscal sponsorship, or a mission-aligned grant; or
- fund a named public deliverable.

Each request should say what success looks like, who will review the work, how
credit is given, and under which contribution terms it will be used.

### 3.4 Ask for financial support without undermining trust

A support page should explain:

1. **What remains free.**
2. **What costs money to sustain.**
3. **What the next funded deliverables are.**
4. **Who receives and controls the funds.**
5. **Whether a contribution is a donation, sponsorship, membership fee, or
   payment for service.**
6. **Whether it is tax-deductible.** Never imply this unless legally true.
7. **Which rights or influence a funder does not receive.**
8. **Where financial reports and conflicts are published.**

Good asks are concrete: fund independent translation review, accessibility
testing, registry moderation, public-interest hosting, security review, a
pilot report, or community participation from an under-resourced region.

The project should not solicit charitable donations into a personal account or
describe ordinary commercial payments as donations. The appropriate entity,
banking, tax, fundraising, and disclosure arrangements depend on jurisdiction
and require professional advice.

## 4. Whom the project can serve

The opportunity is broader than a directory of AI users. Different people need
different forms of accountability.

| Human or institution | Need | Free public value | Possible paid value |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Individual creator | Disclose AI assistance without a paragraph of caveats | Signature, link, declaration, portable profile | Hosted history, private workspace, advanced integrations |
| Reader or recipient | Understand who stands behind a work | Plain-language explanation and basic checker | None required |
| Team | Define who approved which AI-assisted output | Shared templates and public policy format | Roles, approvals, retention, private declarations |
| Organisation | Make policy and responsibility visible across many artifacts | Public organisation profile and self-hosted register | SSO, administration, audit exports, integrations, support |
| Developer | Add the mark to software and metadata | Markdown, HTML, JSON-LD, schema, local checker | CI integrations, high-volume API, service-level support |
| Publisher or platform | Offer consistent disclosure to contributors | Open format and reference implementation | Managed integration and moderation tooling |
| Educator or learner | Turn AI use into a reflection and accountability habit | Classroom guidance and printable templates | Institutional rollout and training |
| Researcher | Study voluntary AI disclosure and adoption | Open, privacy-safe aggregate data | Stable bulk feeds and support, where appropriate |
| Professional body | Test a bounded disclosure practice | Pilot kit and public findings | Facilitated pilot and policy mapping |
| Public-interest group | Improve transparency without surveillance | Open multilingual resources | Funded custom accessibility/localisation work |
| AI vendor | Help users disclose use responsibly | Vendor-neutral inclusion in a taxonomy | No paid preference; only ordinary integration services |
| Translator or community organiser | Make the convention locally meaningful | Open contribution and credit | Stipends or funded community programmes |

The site should remain useful even to a person who never creates an account,
never enters the directory, never reveals a tool, and never pays.

## 5. URL-first identity without pretending a URL proves identity

The author's idea—expressing choices in a URL—is powerful because it is
immediate, portable, cacheable, and requires no database. It should be expanded
carefully.

### 5.1 Four different URL jobs

| URL component | Correct job | Examples of data |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Path** | Identity of a durable resource | profile handle, declaration ID, organisation ID, specification version |
| **Query string** | Presentation and public filtering | language, font, colour, theme, directory sector filter |
| **Fragment** | Client-side state that should not be sent in the HTTP request | an unsigned local draft or compressed portable payload |
| **Manifest linked by the resource** | Structured semantic content | responsible party, scope, AI roles, dates, evidence, revocation |

The path should identify a declaration; the query should decide how it looks.
That gives stable links such as:

- `/p/{handle}` — public profile;
- `/o/{organisation}` — organisation profile;
- `/d/{opaque-declaration-id}` — artifact or scope declaration;
- `/practice/{opaque-register-id}` — AI practice register;
- `/spec/v0.1/` — immutable meaning; and
- `/verify?url={encoded-url}` — an action performed on another resource.

These are conceptual routes, not a requirement to abandon the current static
`.html` pages. Static redirects or generated files can preserve old links.

### 5.2 What should remain in presentation parameters

The existing parameters are appropriate for display:

- `name`
- `company`
- `lang`
- `theme` or display mode
- `font`
- `highlight` or accent colour

They can continue producing an immediate author-controlled explanation. The
page should label the result accurately:

> **Personalised example generated from URL parameters. This page does not
> verify the named party.**

The term **authored link** is better than **verified profile** for this mode.

### 5.3 A no-account declaration builder

A free builder can create three outputs at once:

1. a human-readable personalised explanation link;
2. a portable declaration file or JSON snippet; and
3. copy-ready visible text such as `Name +AI`.

The builder should run entirely in the browser and preserve the repository's
stronger invariant: self-contained, `file://`-safe, zero fetch, and zero build
step for the core free tool. Nothing needs to be uploaded unless the user
explicitly chooses hosted publication or directory listing.

For an artifact-specific declaration it should ask only what is needed:

- responsible party;
- optional organisation;
- artifact or scope;
- AI assistance role or category;
- date;
- specification version;
- optional tool disclosure;
- visibility choice; and
- optional evidence links.

It should not ask for prompts, private source material, API keys, confidential
documents, or an exhaustive interaction transcript.

### 5.4 Fragments are useful but not secret storage

A URL fragment can hold an unsigned portable draft because browsers do not send
the fragment in the normal HTTP request. This enables local-only rendering and
sharing without a database.

It is still not confidential:

- it appears in the visible URL;
- it may remain in browser history or synced tabs;
- scripts on the page can read it;
- recipients can modify it;
- screenshots and copied links expose it; and
- long payloads fail in some clients.

Fragments are suitable for bounded, non-sensitive, self-authored state. Private
data belongs in a local file or an authenticated service with explicit
retention controls.

### 5.5 Canonicalisation rules

Before public links become a long-term interface, define:

- UTF-8 handling and maximum field lengths;
- stable parameter names and aliases;
- deterministic parameter order for canonical links;
- omitted/default-value behaviour;
- case and slug rules;
- collision and rename policy;
- immutable declaration IDs;
- canonical tags for indexed pages;
- specification and manifest-schema versions;
- redirect and archival policy; and
- a rule that unknown parameters are ignored rather than silently changing
  meaning.

Never place email addresses, access tokens, confidential project names, private
tool inventories, or prompts in query strings. Query strings routinely appear
in logs, analytics, support screenshots, browser history, and referrer data.

### 5.6 From authored link to hosted profile

The same portable declaration should be importable into a later account. An
account adds:

- a stable canonical URL;
- edit history;
- revocation and supersession;
- proof-of-control workflows;
- multiple declarations;
- organisation roles; and
- selected public/private visibility.

It does not change the semantic meaning of `+AI`. It only manages records.

## 6. “AI Arsenal”: a promising feature that needs precise semantics

The underlying idea is valuable: let a person or organisation declare the AI
capabilities at their disposal and the practices governing their use.

The phrase **AI Arsenal** is memorable, but it has military, competitive, and
security connotations. That may work as an optional theme or campaign label,
while the durable data object uses neutral language such as:

- **AI Practice Register**
- **AI Toolkit**
- **AI Stack**
- **Tool Disclosure**
- **AI Capability Profile**

The recommended approach is:

> Let users call the view “My AI Arsenal” if they like; define the interoperable
> object as an **AI Practice Register**.

### 6.1 Do not collapse four different claims

These statements must remain separate:

1. **Available to me** — I can access this tool or capability.
2. **Generally used by me** — I use it in some work.
3. **Approved by my organisation** — policy permits it under stated conditions.
4. **Used on this artifact** — it materially assisted this specific work.

A directory profile might publish the first three. Only an artifact declaration
should make the fourth claim.

Without that distinction, a profile listing a tool could falsely imply that it
participated in every subsequent work.

### 6.2 Suggested register fields

Every field should be optional except a stable entry ID and a human-readable
label.

| Field | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Tool/capability name | Human-readable identification |
| Provider | Disambiguation, not endorsement |
| Product/model/version | Optional precision where it matters |
| Capability category | Writing, coding, research, image, translation, agent, local model, and so on |
| Claim type | Available, used generally, approved, or used on this artifact |
| Purpose | What the person or organisation uses it for |
| Human review practice | How outputs are accepted, checked, or escalated |
| Data rule | What may or may not be supplied to the tool |
| Deployment mode | Hosted, local, private tenant, or unknown |
| Effective and review dates | Freshness and historical meaning |
| Public note | Context in the signer's own words |
| Evidence link | Optional policy, declaration, or technical record |
| Visibility | Public, unlisted, organisation-only, or private |
| Status | Active, retired, superseded, disputed, or removed |

The schema should describe capabilities generically enough that it does not
become obsolete whenever a vendor renames a product.

### 6.3 Human-serving uses

An AI practice register could support:

- a personal “tools I use and how I review them” page;
- an organisation's public AI-use policy;
- an internal approved-tool register;
- an artifact-level AI bill of materials;
- onboarding and training;
- procurement comparison;
- team capability discovery;
- finding collaborators with relevant AI experience;
- research into adoption patterns;
- sector-specific guidance;
- change history when tools or policies are retired; and
- safer disclosure of local/private versus public-cloud use.

### 6.4 Free version

The free version should include:

- a no-account builder;
- local save/load;
- portable JSON and human-readable export;
- a shareable authored view;
- self-hosting instructions;
- a basic public hosted register, if hosting is offered;
- accessibility and translation of fixed taxonomy labels; and
- an artifact declaration that references selected register entries.

### 6.5 Premium version

Organisations could pay for:

- private registers;
- multiple teams and administrators;
- approval workflows;
- effective dates and policy acknowledgements;
- SSO and directory synchronisation;
- role-based access;
- controlled taxonomies;
- audit history and retention policy;
- bulk import/export;
- connectors to procurement, developer, and document systems;
- alerts when internal entries need review; and
- implementation and training support.

### 6.6 Risks and safeguards

| Risk | Safeguard |
| --- | --- |
| Tool list becomes advertising | No affiliate links, paid placement, or vendor-written claims presented as user evidence |
| Militarised framing excludes people | Neutral canonical name; “Arsenal” remains an optional presentation |
| Security exposure | Never require exact internal models, endpoints, prompts, data sources, or controls |
| Employer or client confidentiality | Field-level visibility and clear warnings before publication |
| Stale declarations | Visible “last reviewed” date and superseded status |
| Competitive ranking | No universal score for who has the “best” stack |
| Tool implies use on every artifact | Separate profile availability from artifact-specific use |
| Vendor capture of taxonomy | Open, provider-neutral category governance |
| Sensitive inferences | Do not infer tools from browsing, email, source code, or third-party datasets |

The register must be voluntary. `+AI` should never require naming a provider,
model, prompt, or percentage of contribution.

Public directory filters and aggregate exports should default to
provider-neutral capability categories. Vendor/product statistics, if ever
published, need sufficient aggregation, neutral ordering, and an explicit
non-endorsement warning so they cannot function as an official vendor
leaderboard.

## 7. The directory opportunity

The directory can evolve from a signatory wall into useful public
infrastructure, provided it remains opt-in and evidence-literate.

### 7.1 Directory entry types

The directory could contain distinct collections for:

- people;
- organisations;
- teams or projects;
- public AI practice registers;
- artifact declarations;
- institutional pilots;
- integrations and open-source implementations;
- community groups and translators; and
- case studies.

Each collection should state its inclusion rules. Being present should never
mean “approved by +AI.”

### 7.2 Useful discovery facets

Voluntarily supplied filters could include:

- language;
- sector;
- work or artifact type;
- individual, organisation, project, or pseudonymous profile;
- provider-neutral public capability categories;
- country or region at a coarse level, if the signer chooses;
- specification version;
- active, revoked, disputed, or superseded status;
- available evidence types; and
- accessibility or contact preferences.

Avoid filters on protected or highly sensitive personal characteristics.
Do not infer attributes from names, photographs, locations, or activity.

### 7.3 Profile content

A useful public profile might show:

- display name and pronunciation;
- organisation and role, where voluntarily declared;
- a short motivation statement;
- canonical website or social links;
- preferred language;
- public declarations;
- public AI practice register;
- contribution to the +AI project;
- first-declaration and last-reviewed dates;
- evidence labels;
- status and revocation history; and
- an optional contact route that does not expose a private mailbox.

The profile should not become a reputation score. It records claims and
evidence, not a judgement of character.

### 7.4 Visibility modes

At minimum:

1. **Local/private** — exists only on the user's device.
2. **Unlisted** — hosted at an opaque link but not discoverable in the directory.
3. **Public** — included in search and exports.
4. **Organisation-only** — visible to authenticated workspace members.

Moving from a less visible state to a more visible one must require explicit
consent. Search engines should not index unlisted or private views.

### 7.5 Evidence labels, not a single “verified” badge

The directory should report individual facts:

- self-declared;
- mailbox control confirmed;
- account control confirmed;
- domain control confirmed;
- artifact-host control confirmed;
- cryptographic signature valid;
- declaration schema valid;
- specification hash matches;
- last checked on a stated date;
- revoked;
- disputed; or
- removed for abuse/legal reasons.

These facts should not be collapsed into a green tick or numerical trust score.
Domain control does not prove authority to speak for a whole organisation.
Cryptographic integrity does not prove a claim is truthful. A valid declaration
does not prove the artifact is correct.

This recommendation **replaces** the green/amber/red tier proposed in
`core-extensions.md` §1.4. Registry presence and active status must be reported
as separate facts, never as an upgrade to a higher trust colour or tier.

### 7.6 Directory business opportunities that remain ethical

The directory itself should not sell rank. Revenue can sit beside it:

- private organisation directories;
- team administration;
- saved internal views;
- high-volume synchronisation;
- integration support;
- domain portfolio management;
- managed moderation for an organisation's own private space;
- evidence export;
- custom policy taxonomy; and
- service-level guarantees for APIs.

Basic public inclusion, correction, revocation, and evidence visibility should
not depend on payment.

### 7.7 Higher-risk extensions

The data could later support:

- professional discovery;
- collaboration or mentor matching;
- job and project opportunities;
- a marketplace of implementation providers;
- sector benchmarking; or
- procurement discovery.

These are possible but not early priorities. They introduce discrimination,
ranking, advertising, employment, fraud, and marketplace-liability concerns.
If built, they should be separate opt-in products with transparent ordering and
no implication that directory presence proves competence.

### 7.8 The directory as open research infrastructure

A privacy-safe export could help answer:

- Which sectors use voluntary AI disclosure?
- Which languages and artifact types are underserved?
- Which review practices are common?
- How often are declarations repeated, superseded, or revoked?
- Does the mark change behaviour in bounded pilots?

Publish only deliberately public fields and sufficiently aggregated statistics.
An open licence does not remove the ethical duty to minimise doxxing and abuse.
Bulk personal data should not be exposed merely because a technical export is
easy. Public research exports should use provider-neutral capability
categories by default rather than vendor-level rankings.

## 8. Artifact declarations and “passports”

The profile says who someone is. The declaration says what they stand behind.

### 8.1 Minimal declaration

A minimal declaration needs:

- declaration ID;
- specification version;
- responsible party;
- artifact or scope;
- date issued;
- visible `+AI` notation; and
- status.

### 8.2 Optional detail

It may also contain:

- organisation;
- roles of AI assistance;
- selected practice-register entries;
- content or manifest hash;
- source and derivative relationships;
- human review statement;
- jurisdiction- or sector-specific supplemental disclosure;
- evidence of control;
- signature information;
- supersedes/superseded-by links; and
- revocation or dispute information.

### 8.3 Lifecycle

A declaration should be immutable once issued, subject to the privacy, safety,
and legal exceptions in §17.3. Corrections normally create a successor; they do
not rewrite history. Status events can record:

- issued;
- superseded;
- revoked by signer;
- disputed;
- removed from public display;
- withdrawn for privacy or legal reasons; and
- archived.

Where personal data must be erased, retain only the minimum lawful and
non-identifying tombstone needed to avoid silently reusing an ID. “Append-only”
is an engineering preference, not permission to ignore privacy or safety law.

### 8.4 Hashes and dynamic artifacts

A content hash is useful only when its scope is defined. Dynamic pages contain
timestamps, advertisements, personalisation, or changing data and may never
produce the same whole-page hash twice.

Options include:

- hash a downloadable immutable artifact;
- hash a declared DOM region;
- hash canonical text;
- hash a C2PA manifest;
- record a source-system version ID; or
- omit the hash and say that the declaration is scope-based, not byte-bound.

The checker should distinguish **identity/control evidence**, **schema
validity**, **specification integrity**, and **content binding** rather than
announcing one undifferentiated verdict.

### 8.5 Certificates and QR

A printable certificate and QR code can make declarations useful in:

- reports;
- proposals;
- packaging;
- exhibitions;
- conference posters;
- education;
- compliance files; and
- offline archives.

The certificate should always point back to a status resource. A PDF can be
copied or become stale; it is an export, not the source of truth.

## 9. Email: five distinct possibilities

Email is likely to be one of the strongest adoption channels, but the visible
mark and an address containing `+ai` are different concepts.

### 9.1 Visible email signature

The simplest and most valuable use is:

> **Eric Mourant +AI**

Hyperlink `+AI` or the whole signature to a personalised explanation or
declaration. Always provide a plain-text form because images are blocked,
signatures are reformatted, and accessibility matters.

The free toolkit should generate instructions for common mail clients, a small
badge, plain text, and a privacy-safe link.

### 9.2 Plus-addressed mailbox

An address such as `eric.mourant+ai@japer.technology` may route to
`eric.mourant@japer.technology` **if the receiving domain is configured to
support subaddressing**.

Important realities:

- `+tag` behaviour is controlled by the receiving mail system.
- Support, filtering, forwarding, and canonicalisation are not universal.
- The tag is not a separate human identity or security credential.
- The base mailbox is usually easy to infer.
- Some web forms incorrectly reject otherwise valid plus signs.
- A tag must not be used to defeat account or rate limits.
- The exact address should be preserved; software must not universally strip
  plus tags because local-part semantics belong to the receiving domain.
- If an address is included inside a query parameter, the literal plus must be
  encoded as `%2B`, or form-style decoding may turn it into a space.

This can be a useful personal filtering convention or campaign signal. It
should not be required and should not be treated as proof of a `+AI`
declaration.

### 9.3 Privacy-preserving reply relay

A later service could provide an address such as
`reply+{opaque-id}@plus-artificial-intelligence.org` that forwards to a signer
without publishing the signer's mailbox.

Free or paid uses could include:

- contact from a declaration page;
- expiring aliases;
- spam controls;
- per-profile blocking; and
- organisation-managed routing.

It creates substantial operational duties: abuse handling, deliverability,
malware filtering, bounce management, data retention, sender authentication,
and the risk that the project appears to endorse the sender or recipient.
It should be optional, use opaque identifiers, and avoid open/click tracking.

### 9.4 Declaration by email

A future workflow could receive a message at a special address and return a
draft declaration or archive receipt. This is attractive for ordinary users but
risky:

- the From header can be spoofed;
- forwarded content may contain confidential data;
- attachments create malware and storage risks;
- replies can accidentally publish private threads; and
- an automated agent cannot decide that the sender accepts responsibility.

If explored, require authenticated mail, explicit confirmation, narrow
retention, and a preview before publication. Do not make inbound email an
automatic public declaration.

### 9.5 Email metadata

A custom header could carry a structured reference, but unknown headers may be
removed by clients and gateways. It can supplement—not replace—the visible
signature. Any future header proposal should be versioned, documented openly,
and tested for interoperability before being described as a standard.

### 9.6 Recommendation

Ship the visible signature kit now. Document plus-addressing as an optional
mailbox convention. Defer relays, inbound declaration processing, and custom
headers until there is demand and operational capacity.

## 11. What should always be free

Serving humans for free is not just a zero-price tier. It is a design
commitment to agency, accessibility, privacy, portability, and non-coercion.

### 11.1 Proposed Free Floor v1—the source of truth

This section is the source of truth for free-versus-paid boundaries in this
document; later tables and red-line lists summarise it. Before hosted services
launch, the commons custodian should ratify and publish it as a versioned
commitment.

Free Floor v1 should guarantee:

- the canonical definition and versioned specification;
- all reviewed translations;
- plain-language explanations and examples;
- use of the `+AI` notation;
- all existing public themes;
- accessible neutral presentation;
- personalised authored links;
- no-account declaration and practice-register builders;
- local save/load and portable exports;
- basic badges, signatures, QR codes, and snippets;
- self-hosting documentation;
- public schemas and conformance examples;
- offline pack;
- unlimited client-side/manual checking and free self-service issuance of every
  evidence label the project supports, including mailbox, DNS,
  `/.well-known`, file-challenge, and signature checks;
- a basic public profile, public declarations, and directory inclusion without
  a fee when an applicant meets the same published safety and admission policy
  as everyone else;
- correction, revocation, privacy, and abuse-reporting processes;
- a complete versioned public-registry snapshot, without an account, emitted
  for each published registry change;
- if a hosted verification proxy exists, at least 20 checks per client per day,
  alongside the unlimited client-side checker;
- if a live public read API exists, at least 100 cached reads per client per
  day, alongside the complete static snapshot;
- changelog and status history;
- educational and pilot materials; and
- public governance, funding, and transparency reports.

The custodian may apply proportionate anti-abuse throttles, but not use them to
make ordinary human use impractical or to favour paying parties. A capability
in a ratified free floor may be replaced only by an equal or more open route,
not removed. Permanent changes require a published rationale, custodian
approval, archived prior version, and at least 90 days' notice; temporary
security restrictions require prompt public explanation when disclosure is
safe.

### 11.2 Human-centred design commitments

The free service should:

- work without an account for the core action;
- work on low-cost devices and constrained connections;
- preserve file/offline use;
- offer a non-decorative, high-contrast presentation;
- support keyboard, screen-reader, reduced-motion, CJK, and RTL use;
- avoid manipulative sign-up prompts;
- collect nothing unless needed for a requested feature;
- let users publish anonymously or pseudonymously where appropriate;
- not require naming an AI vendor;
- not shame people who do not use the mark;
- not tell a person that a machine has accepted responsibility for them;
- make export and exit easy; and
- give the 40-language audience meaningful participation in governance, not
  only translated marketing.

### 11.3 Public-benefit programmes

Funding can expand free access through:

- translator and reviewer stipends;
- accessibility audits;
- community educator grants;
- free implementation clinics;
- open pilot reports;
- templates for schools, libraries, nonprofits, and small organisations;
- security and privacy reviews;
- long-term archiving; and
- fee waivers or sponsored premium access for public-interest organisations.

This is how revenue can strengthen rather than hollow out the commons.

## 13. Funding the commons

No single revenue source is sufficient or neutral in every circumstance.
Diversification protects the mission.

| Source | Strength | Principal risk | Control |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Individual contributions | Community legitimacy | Volatility and processing overhead | Recurring option, transparent use of funds |
| Fiscal sponsor | Fast route to compliant nonprofit administration | Sponsor dependence and fees | Written scope, exit and asset-transfer terms |
| Foundation/public-interest grants | Funds open work that customers may not | Funder-driven priorities | Mission screen and public grant terms |
| Corporate sponsorship | Can fund infrastructure and events | Perceived endorsement or vendor capture | No product preference, disclosed limits |
| Membership | Predictable support and stakeholder participation | Pay-to-govern dominance | Free individual use, voting caps, community seats |
| Paid pilots | Validates real organisational needs | Bespoke consulting can consume the roadmap | Bounded scope and publish reusable learning |
| Hosted subscriptions | Recurring earned revenue | Pressure to enclose the open layer | Open exports and a constitutional free floor |
| Training/support | Aligned with adoption | Founder-time bottleneck | Train additional facilitators |
| Custom integrations/themes | Clear customer value | Distracts from core infrastructure | Price fully and separate from standards work |
| In-kind support | Reduces hosting, legal, and design cost | Hidden dependence | Disclose material support and preserve portability |

### 13.1 Donation and supporter page

The page should include:

- current legal recipient;
- whether gifts are tax-deductible;
- payment processor and privacy information;
- one-time and recurring options;
- non-financial ways to help;
- a public budget or funded-deliverables list;
- supporter-recognition choices, including anonymity;
- refund/contact policy;
- sponsor independence statement; and
- latest financial/transparency report.

Avoid a donor wall that looks like a ranking of trusted signers. Recognise
support in a separate context from the directory.

### 13.2 Sponsorship policy

Sponsors may receive factual recognition such as “supported translation review”
or “funded accessibility testing.” They should not receive:

- control of the specification;
- preferential evidence status;
- directory rank;
- exclusivity over a tool category;
- access to user data;
- a claim that `+AI` recommends their product; or
- the right to suppress unfavourable pilot findings.

### 13.3 Membership

Membership can fund a future custodian and working groups, but membership must
not be required to use the mark. A balanced structure might include:

- free community participation;
- low-cost individual supporting membership;
- organisational membership;
- sponsored membership for under-resourced groups; and
- voting safeguards so one funding class cannot dominate.

### 13.4 Grants and public deliverables

Good grant targets include:

- translation quality;
- accessibility;
- public-interest AI literacy;
- content provenance interoperability;
- digital public infrastructure;
- open-source tooling;
- accountable AI adoption research; and
- support for schools, libraries, media, and civil society.

Grant-funded outputs should remain openly available unless privacy or safety
requires otherwise.

## 14. Stewardship and organisational structures

The long-term credibility question is not merely “Who owns the domain?” It is
“Who can change the meaning, control the registry, spend the money, and resolve
disputes?”

### 14.1 Structural options

| Model | Advantages | Disadvantages | Best use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Founder-led personal project | Fast and coherent | Bus factor, weak continuity, donation and neutrality concerns | Earliest experimentation |
| Project within JAPER Technology | Existing commercial capacity | The public convention may look proprietary | Paid prototypes and services |
| Fiscal sponsorship | Faster nonprofit administration and grant access | Dependency on sponsor terms | Transitional commons stewardship |
| Independent nonprofit/association/foundation | Mission lock, neutral custody, broad governance | Setup and compliance overhead | Long-term standard and public assets |
| Standards consortium | Institutional adoption and shared maintenance | Slow, membership capture risk | Later interoperability work |
| Dual structure | Neutral custodian plus commercial service providers | Requires strict conflicts and contracts | Recommended mature model |

### 14.2 Recommended direction

Use a staged dual structure:

1. **Commons custodian** — holds or stewards the specification, canonical
   definition, public schemas, project identity, public registry policy, and
   mission-locked assets.
2. **Community** — signers, translators, maintainers, sector groups, pilot
   participants, and independent critics.
3. **Service providers** — JAPER Technology and potentially others may offer
   optional hosting, integration, training, and consulting on equal published
   terms.

Initially, fiscal sponsorship may achieve the separation faster than forming a
new entity. The correct legal vehicle depends on jurisdiction, charitable
purpose, tax, governance, fundraising, and operating plans.

### 14.3 Governance components

A credible governance system eventually needs:

- public mission and values;
- free-use guarantee;
- specification change process;
- versioning and decision records;
- maintainer roles and succession;
- community consultation;
- translation governance;
- registry admission and evidence policy;
- moderation, takedown, and appeal process;
- privacy and child-safety policy;
- funding and sponsor policy;
- conflict-of-interest register and recusals;
- security reporting and incident response;
- annual transparency report; and
- a process for changing the governance itself.

The founder can retain a clearly defined originator or steward role without
holding unilateral permanent control over every function.

### 14.4 Separate powers where practical

The same person should not indefinitely:

- sell a premium service;
- decide the public evidence rules;
- resolve complaints against that service;
- control directory visibility; and
- change the canonical meaning.

At small scale, complete separation is unrealistic. Publish conflicts, record
decisions, use recusal where possible, and add independent participants as the
impact grows.

### 14.5 Licensing and contributions

Obtain legal advice on a coherent package such as:

- permissive open-source licence for code;
- open content licence for documentation and translations;
- clear terms for registry data;
- Developer Certificate of Origin or contributor agreement;
- trademark/project-name policy;
- canonical mark-use guidelines; and
- a defensive-publication record of the defined responsibility meaning and
  prior-art research.

The mark-use policy should protect free genuine use while prohibiting false
claims of project endorsement, certification, or exclusive ownership.

The legal posture should distinguish two objects explicitly:

- the `+AI` notation in its canonical sense receives a free-use,
  non-assertion-style covenant consistent with specification §19; and
- the project name, logo, and any future distinct certification credential may
  receive ordinary trademark protection against confusion or false
  endorsement.

Guidelines for the notation may explain accurate use, but should not claim
exclusive ownership of the characters.

### 14.6 Certification is a separate decision

The plain mark is a self-declaration. A future independent conformance or
assurance programme is possible, but it would require:

- published criteria;
- competent and independent assessors;
- consistent decisions;
- appeals;
- surveillance/renewal rules;
- liability analysis;
- accessible pricing or fee support; and
- a visually and semantically distinct credential.

It should never redefine ordinary `+AI` as the “unverified inferior version.”
The project can thrive without offering certification at all.

### 14.7 Continuity and namespace custody

Every email signature, badge, QR code, certificate, and declaration depends on
the domain continuing to resolve. The custodian—not an individual founder or a
commercial service provider—should ultimately hold the apex domain, canonical
namespace, signing keys, and archival responsibility.

A continuity commitment should provide:

- protected registrar access, renewal, recovery, and documented succession;
- immutable handling of issued `/d/{id}` identifiers;
- no reuse of retired declaration IDs;
- at least 180 days' public notice before a planned hosted-service shutdown;
- a final static export of public profiles, declarations, schemas, status
  history, and verification instructions;
- transfer or escrow arrangements allowing a successor custodian to preserve
  links;
- read-only resolution of historical declarations after writes stop; and
- a public emergency plan for loss of the domain or signing keys.

Commercial customer data needs separate export and deletion arrangements, but
the public namespace should not disappear with a vendor.

## 15. A technical architecture that preserves the static commons

Accounts and a database do not need to replace the current architecture.

### 15.1 Layered model

```text
Static commons
  specification · translations · themes · builders · schemas · offline tools
              │
              ▼
Public signed/static exports
  registry snapshots · profiles · declarations · changelog · feeds
              │
              ▼
Optional hosted service
  accounts · write API · moderation · organisation workspaces · billing
              │
              ▼
Integrations
  CMS · Git · documents · email · C2PA · enterprise systems
```

If the hosted service stops operating, the specification, builders, schemas,
exports, existing declarations, and self-hosting path should remain useful.

### 15.2 Core records

Keep these concepts separate in the data model:

- **Party** — person, pseudonym, organisation, team, or project;
- **Profile** — chosen public context for a party;
- **Organisation membership/authority** — who may act for which organisation;
- **Practice register** — general capabilities, policies, and tool entries;
- **Declaration** — adoption of responsibility for a scope/artifact;
- **Artifact** — external or stored immutable object being referenced;
- **Evidence** — domain, mailbox, signature, hash, and consistency observations;
- **Status event** — issue, supersede, revoke, dispute, redact, or remove;
- **Consent/visibility** — what may be public and indexed;
- **Moderation case** — reports, evidence, decisions, and appeals; and
- **Entitlement/billing** — paid service rights.

Billing must not be stored as or translated into an evidence fact. This
structural separation helps prevent accidental pay-to-trust behaviour.

### 15.3 Read architecture

Public reads should be:

- unauthenticated;
- cacheable;
- available as versioned static JSON;
- accompanied by ETags or hashes;
- paginated for large collections;
- incrementally synchronisable;
- documented with a stable schema; and
- mirrorable.

The database can be the operational write store while an event-driven or
scheduled process emits deterministic public snapshots. The public website need
not query the live database for every page view.

### 15.4 Write architecture

Hosted writes require:

- proven account control;
- scoped authorisation;
- organisation role checks;
- immutable IDs;
- append-only status history with privacy exceptions;
- rate limits;
- anti-automation controls;
- moderation queues;
- audit logging;
- safe file handling;
- notifications for material changes; and
- recovery from compromised accounts.

Use an established identity provider or well-tested authentication framework
rather than inventing password or cryptographic identity infrastructure.

### 15.5 Public API

Potential endpoints include:

- registry/profile lookup;
- declaration lookup;
- filtered public search;
- changes since a cursor;
- schema and specification version discovery;
- verification of a declaration or URL;
- authenticated publication/revocation; and
- webhooks for organisation or integration events.

Version the API from its first public release. Additive fields are safer than
mutating existing meanings. High-volume access can be paid, but a static
low-volume/open-data path should remain.

### 15.6 Verification service safety

A server-side checker that fetches user-supplied URLs can be abused for
server-side request forgery, internal-network probing, denial of service, and
malware retrieval. It needs:

- allowed HTTP/HTTPS schemes only;
- DNS and IP checks before every connection and redirect, with the validated
  address pinned for the connection to prevent DNS-rebinding races;
- blocking of loopback, link-local, private, and metadata-service ranges;
- strict redirect, response-size, and time limits;
- content-type restrictions;
- no browser execution of fetched scripts;
- caching and rate limits; and
- sanitised, non-reflective error output.

A client-side checker avoids some server risk but is constrained by browser
CORS rules. A safe hosted proxy is an optional service, not a reason to weaken
network controls.

### 15.7 Cost discipline

Keep the free layer sustainable by:

- generating static pages and snapshots;
- caching immutable versions aggressively;
- keeping media optional;
- using queued background validation;
- limiting free automated verification volume while retaining manual checks;
- avoiding per-view database queries;
- publishing an offline pack; and
- measuring operational cost per declaration, profile, and API client.

## 16. Trust without a trust score

The project should expose evidence, not manufacture an aura.

### 16.1 Separate questions

A checker should answer independently:

1. Is the declaration syntactically valid?
2. Which schema and specification version does it use?
3. Does the linked explanation match the expected specification hash?
4. Does the declarant control the account, mailbox, domain, or artifact location
   claimed?
5. Is a cryptographic signature valid and from which key?
6. Is the artifact bound to the declaration?
7. Is the declaration active, superseded, revoked, disputed, or removed?
8. When was each fact last checked?

It should not answer “Is this person good?” or “Is this work true?”

### 16.2 Proof of control is not proof of authority

Different proofs establish different things:

- mailbox confirmation proves access to a mailbox at a moment;
- OAuth proves control of an account under the provider's rules;
- DNS or `/.well-known` proves control of a domain;
- a file challenge proves control of a page or repository;
- a signature proves possession of a key; and
- organisation authority requires governance beyond simple domain access.

Use accurate phrases such as **domain control confirmed**, not **verified
organisation**, unless actual authority has been established.

### 16.3 No paid evidence outcome

Payment may buy a managed workflow, support, rapid rechecking, or an independent
assessment. It may not:

- hide adverse status;
- skip evidence requirements;
- turn self-declared into domain-controlled;
- buy a green badge;
- remove a legitimate dispute; or
- move a profile above others in “most trustworthy” order.

“Rapid rechecking” means scheduled or proactive technical monitoring. Public
correction, revocation, impersonation, privacy, safety, and dispute reports must
share the same published service targets and queue rules for paying and
non-paying parties; money cannot buy priority over the integrity of the public
record.

## 17. Privacy, security, safety, and abuse

The directory and account service create much more risk than the current static
site. Policies and controls must arrive before open self-service publication.

### 17.1 Threat and safeguard map

| Threat | Why it matters | Minimum safeguard |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Impersonation | False responsibility can harm people and organisations | Proof-of-control labels, report flow, rapid dispute state |
| Organisation overclaim | Domain user may not be authorised to represent the whole entity | Role/authority process and precise wording |
| URL data leakage | Queries appear in logs, history, and referrers | No sensitive fields in URLs; bounded public parameters |
| Stored/reflected XSS | Profiles contain user-supplied text and links | Output encoding, URL allow-listing, sanitisation, CSP |
| Malicious outbound links | Directory can become an SEO/phishing farm | Link safety, moderation, `rel` controls, no paid backlinks |
| Checker SSRF | Verification fetches arbitrary URLs | Network isolation and strict fetch policy |
| Spam and mass registration | Destroys discovery and increases cost | Rate limits, verification, duplicate detection, moderation |
| Account takeover | Attacker can alter or revoke declarations | MFA options, session security, alerts, recovery, event history |
| Doxxing/scraping | Public profiles can expose vulnerable people | Minimal fields, unlisted mode, no public email by default |
| Sensitive stack disclosure | “Arsenal” can reveal security or commercial information | Voluntary fields, warnings, private registers |
| Stale claims | Tools, roles, and domains change | Review dates, expiry cues, status history |
| Surveillance analytics | Contradicts a human-serving trust project | No ad-tech/fingerprinting; aggregate opt-in measurement |
| Secret/prompt exposure | Builders may tempt users to paste private material | Explicit exclusions, client-side processing, no prompt field |
| Child exposure | Public identity and “responsibility” language may be unsafe | No public child profile by default; specific education policy |
| Defamation/illegal content | Directory hosts user claims | Terms, notice-and-action, evidence preservation, appeals |
| Premium capture | Paying customers influence public truth | Governance separation and public conflict register |

### 17.2 Data minimisation

Public registration should not require:

- date of birth;
- precise location;
- legal identity where a pseudonym is appropriate;
- personal email display;
- government ID;
- biometric proof;
- contacts;
- browsing history;
- prompts;
- private documents; or
- inferred tool usage.

Collect private account data only for a stated purpose and retention period.
Keep public registry fields distinct from private authentication and billing
records.

### 17.3 Append-only versus erasure

The roadmap's append-only principle protects accountability, but absolute
retention can harm an impersonated person, a vulnerable individual, or someone
with a valid legal erasure right.

A defensible policy is:

- immutable IDs and status history by default;
- revocation rather than silent disappearance for legitimate public
  declarations;
- redaction of nonessential personal fields;
- hard deletion where legally required or where an entry was never authorised;
- a minimal non-identifying tombstone when safe and lawful; and
- published aggregate counts of removals and reasons.

This needs jurisdiction-specific privacy advice before launch.

### 17.4 Moderation

Before opening public submission, publish:

- admission rules;
- prohibited content;
- impersonation process;
- safety and privacy reporting;
- notice-and-action procedure;
- evidence handling;
- status vocabulary;
- decision authority;
- response targets;
- appeal route; and
- transparency reporting.

Automate objective checks, not moral judgement. Human review should focus on
impersonation, disputes, illegal material, harassment, and edge cases.

### 17.5 Children and education

Children can learn the disclosure habit without being placed in a public
directory. Recommended defaults:

- the visible notation remains usable in school work;
- student guidance treats it as reflection and disclosure, not a legal waiver;
- no public minor profile without a deliberately designed guardian/institution
  process;
- no behavioural profiling of learners;
- schools can use private class or institution registers; and
- child-safety reports receive a dedicated route.

Age and consent rules vary by jurisdiction. Avoid collecting child data unless
the project has the expertise and resources to protect it.

### 17.6 Legal and policy work

Before hosted accounts, payments, or a public directory, obtain appropriate
professional advice and publish, as applicable:

- terms of use;
- privacy notice;
- cookie/analytics notice;
- acceptable-use policy;
- registry and evidence policy;
- moderation/takedown/appeals policy;
- child-safety policy;
- trademark and mark-use guidelines;
- contributor terms;
- sponsor and conflict policy;
- security disclosure policy;
- accessibility statement;
- refund/subscription terms; and
- fundraising disclosures.

This document is strategic exploration, not legal advice.

## 18. Forty languages and accessibility are product infrastructure

The present language breadth should shape every new service.

### 18.1 Separate three content types

1. **Normative content** — specification meaning; requires controlled,
   human-reviewed translation.
2. **Interface/taxonomy content** — buttons, filters, sectors, capability
   categories, statuses; centrally translated and versioned.
3. **User content** — names, biographies, organisation text, practice notes;
   displayed as entered with correct language and direction metadata.

Do not machine-translate user content silently or treat a translated interface
as proof that a profile's claims were reviewed in that language.

### 18.2 Translation governance

The project needs:

- language maintainers or review groups;
- visible translation status and source version;
- an easy correction process;
- a glossary for normative and evidence terms;
- support for regional variants where meaning requires it;
- funding for difficult or underserved reviews;
- community credit; and
- fallbacks that never disguise untranslated normative text as reviewed.

### 18.3 Accessibility floor

All core routes—about, help, support, builder, profile, directory, checker, and
account settings—should have a neutral WCAG-oriented presentation independent
of decorative themes.

Required considerations include:

- keyboard operation;
- visible focus;
- screen-reader names and status announcements;
- reduced motion;
- contrast;
- reflow and zoom;
- plain-language summaries;
- print;
- CJK font coverage;
- RTL layout;
- language tags on mixed-language content;
- non-colour evidence/status cues; and
- accessible CAPTCHA alternatives.

Premium themes must never be the only accessible route.

## 19. The complete opportunity horizon

Not every possibility should be built. Mapping the horizon helps keep later
choices deliberate.

### 19.1 Foundation: directly aligned

- founder, motivation, help, support, governance, and contact pages;
- email signature and link kit;
- no-account personalised declaration;
- AI practice-register prototype;
- open schema and local export;
- versioned specification and hashes;
- public usage guidelines;
- opt-in signatory registry;
- basic checker;
- badges, QR, certificates, and platform snippets;
- pilot and case-study programme; and
- transparent funding.

### 19.2 Product layer: high potential

- stable hosted profiles;
- artifact passports;
- organisation profiles and public AI policies;
- private organisation workspaces;
- approved-tool/practice registers;
- roles and approval workflows;
- integrations with publishing, code, documents, and email;
- domain and artifact-control evidence;
- static open-data exports;
- high-volume API and webhooks;
- accessibility/localisation services;
- implementation training; and
- managed pilots.

### 19.3 Ecosystem layer: valuable after adoption

- browser and CMS-native indicators;
- C2PA/IPTC mappings;
- content-credential tooling;
- professional-body implementation guides;
- education curriculum;
- public-interest procurement templates;
- sector communities;
- research observatory;
- federation and mirrors;
- contributor and translator funding;
- annual accountability report;
- independent conformance implementations; and
- standards-body engagement.

### 19.4 Agentic future: plausible extensions

As AI agents act with delegated authority, the project could explore:

- declarations that name the human or organisation delegating authority;
- bounded agent scopes and expiry;
- chains of human and machine contribution;
- who approved autonomous publication or deployment;
- agent-action receipts;
- revocation of delegated authority;
- multi-party responsibility;
- machine-readable escalation contacts; and
- links between an agent action and the human decision to release its result.

These extensions should not be folded casually into v0.1. The simple mark must
remain understandable before a more complex delegation grammar is introduced.

### 19.5 Possibilities to approach cautiously

- professional marketplace;
- job matching;
- public comparative rankings;
- paid process assurance;
- certification;
- hosted email relay;
- public comments or social feed;
- reputation or trust scoring;
- automated inference of AI use;
- tool-vendor sponsorship;
- blockchain anchoring; and
- government or employment identity integration.

Each adds regulatory, discrimination, safety, moderation, or capture risk that
can exceed its value.

### 19.6 Possibilities to reject as core requirements

The following are consequences of Free Floor v1 in §11.1, not a separate
boundary that may drift from it:

- payment to use `+AI`;
- mandatory registration;
- mandatory public identity;
- mandatory disclosure of tools or prompts;
- paid directory placement;
- selling personal data or leads;
- affiliate-ranked tool listings;
- covert tracking or fingerprinting;
- biometric proof of humanity;
- one universal credibility score;
- blockchain as a prerequisite;
- an approved-vendor list that changes the mark's meaning;
- automated publication in someone else's name; and
- any claim that `+AI` transfers responsibility to a machine.

## 20. Recommended sequence

The order matters more than the number of features.

### 20.1 Relationship to the existing plans

Where this document conflicts with earlier conceptual sequencing, its
privacy/safety gates, evidence-label model, and Free Floor v1 are the
recommended replacements. The documents map as follows:

| This document | `THE-PLAN.md` | `core-extensions.md` | Reconciliation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Stage 1 | Foundation and launch-stack content | Pre-Phase 0 governance prerequisite | Add the missing human, licence, funding, and policy layer |
| Stage 2 | Practical kit | Prototype subset of Phase 2 Adoption | Local/no-account testing only; no trust claims |
| Stage 3 | Founding Signatories plus infrastructure | Phase 0 Foundations + Phase 1 Trust | Versioning, policies, registry, and factual checker ship together |
| Stage 4 | Beyond the current campaign plan | New account layer after Phase 1 | Accounts manage records but do not define the mark |
| Stage 5 | Paid bounded pilots | Optional services beside Phases 2–3 | Revenue cannot change public evidence or rank |
| Stage 6 | Institutional expansion | Phases 3–4 Ecosystem/Standards | Interoperate and broaden stewardship after real adoption |

The launch-period Founding Signatories wall may exist before Stage 3 only as a
small, curated, manually reviewed list with no self-service publication and no
evidence tiers. Opening a general submission path waits for the Stage 3
policies and controls.

### Stage 1 — Make the project human and governable

- publish Why, About Eric, Help Build, Support, Governance, and Contact;
- state the free public floor and non-endorsement rule;
- decide licences and contribution terms;
- publish funding and conflict principles;
- identify concrete contribution needs;
- define project versus JAPER Technology roles; and
- make the origin and succession intention explicit.

These non-normative project pages may initially ship in English with a
prominent translation-status label and an open call for translation. They are
outside the normative specification review pipeline and must never be presented
as reviewed normative text; prioritised human review should then extend them
across the existing language community.

**Gate:** A visitor can identify the originator, motivation, maintainer, free-use
promise, contribution route, and funding recipient without reading the
specification.

### Stage 2 — Prove the URL-first idea without accounts

- extend the toolkit concept into a declaration/practice-register prototype;
- keep processing local;
- generate human-readable, JSON, metadata, and QR outputs;
- label authored links as self-authored, not verified;
- test “AI Arsenal” against neutral alternative names;
- publish email signature and plus-addressing guidance; and
- conduct accessibility, privacy, and multilingual usability review.

**Gate:** People unfamiliar with the project can create, understand, export,
and later reopen a declaration without surrendering data.

### Stage 3 — Pilot an opt-in public registry

- version the specification and schema;
- publish registry inclusion, evidence, moderation, privacy, and appeal rules;
- begin with reviewed submissions or repository-based changes;
- use stable declaration IDs and static exports;
- show factual evidence labels;
- support unlisted use and revocation; and
- run bounded pilots across several sectors and languages.

**Gate:** Impersonation, correction, revocation, privacy requests, and disputes
can be handled consistently before open self-service.

### Stage 4 — Add hosted accounts only where they remove friction

- provide profile management and declaration history;
- use established authentication;
- separate account PII from public records;
- add domain/artifact-control workflows;
- add organisation roles;
- preserve no-account and self-hosted routes;
- export deterministic public snapshots; and
- maintain complete portability.

**Gate:** Users choose accounts for convenience, not because the commons has
been degraded.

### Stage 5 — Introduce premium organisation services

- private workspaces and practice registers;
- SSO and team administration;
- integrations;
- higher-volume APIs;
- evidence and retention exports;
- training, pilots, and dedicated support; and
- managed custom deployment.

**Gate:** Revenue demonstrably funds sustainable operations and public
infrastructure without changing evidence outcomes or public rank.

### Stage 6 — Interoperate and distribute stewardship

- C2PA/IPTC/schema crosswalks;
- browser/CMS/tool integrations;
- federation and archive policy;
- formal working groups;
- broader independent governance;
- annual adoption/integrity/funding reports; and
- possible standards-track engagement.

**Gate:** Adoption and maintenance capacity justify the complexity.

## 21. Experiments that answer the important questions

Before building a large account platform, run small tests:

1. Can a new visitor explain `+AI` accurately after ten seconds?
2. Does a founder page increase trust or make the idea feel proprietary?
3. Which call to action produces real use: copy the mark, build a link, create
   a declaration, or join a directory?
4. Do people understand the difference between an authored link and a verified
   control fact?
5. Do users want to disclose tools, practices, both, or neither?
6. Is “AI Arsenal” motivating, confusing, or alienating across cultures and
   sectors?
7. Which practice fields are genuinely useful and safe to publish?
8. Will people maintain a register after the first week?
9. Do readers use detailed declarations or only the six-word explanation?
10. Can organisations complete a bounded pilot without bespoke software?
11. Which operational pain creates willingness to pay?
12. Can moderation handle false listings and disputes at pilot scale?
13. Do the 40-language interfaces produce equivalent understanding?
14. Does the mark change review or accountability behaviour, not merely
    disclosure appearance?

Publish methods and negative findings. A project about responsibility gains
credibility by showing what did not work.

## 22. Metrics that do not require surveillance

### 22.1 Human outcomes

- accurate understanding of the mark;
- percentage of declarations tied to real artifacts;
- repeat use;
- reported usefulness to signers and readers;
- improved review/disclosure behaviour in pilots;
- successful correction and revocation;
- language and accessibility coverage; and
- user ability to export or self-host.

### 22.2 Adoption

- distinct voluntary signers and organisations;
- real artifacts carrying the notation;
- public and unlisted declarations created;
- integrations and independent implementations;
- institutional pilots;
- community contributions;
- reviewed translations; and
- independent references to the canonical meaning.

### 22.3 Integrity and safety

- evidence types present;
- stale or broken declarations;
- impersonation/dispute reports;
- median response and resolution time;
- revocations, redactions, and legal removals;
- security/privacy incidents;
- appeal outcomes; and
- child-safety reports.

### 22.4 Sustainability

- unrestricted versus restricted revenue;
- recurring earned revenue;
- cost per hosted profile/declaration/check;
- funding concentration;
- volunteer and paid maintainer capacity;
- public-good expenditure;
- sponsor/conflict disclosures; and
- service customer retention.

Avoid counting unique people through fingerprinting. Use voluntary registry
records, aggregate server data with short retention, public artifacts, surveys,
and bounded pilot measurements.

## 24. Definitive recommendation

Build `+AI` as three connected layers:

### The commons

The free notation, meaning, specification, translations, themes, builders,
portable schemas, self-hosting, basic checker, and public governance.

### The voluntary public record

Authored links, declarations, profiles, AI practice registers, artifact
passports, factual control/integrity evidence, and an opt-in directory.

### The service layer

Organisation workflows, private registers, roles, SSO, integrations, bulk APIs,
managed hosting, evidence exports, training, pilots, and support.

Make the human story visible now. Invite help in specific forms. Accept
financial support only through transparent structures. Use URL parameters for
presentation and no-account creation, stable paths for durable public
resources, and manifests for semantics. Treat “AI Arsenal” as an optional,
memorable view over a precise and neutral AI Practice Register. Keep email
signatures simple; treat plus-addressing as a provider-dependent convenience,
not identity.

Most importantly, do not monetise the one thing that gives the project value:
the ability of any person to say, freely and plainly:

# **AI helped. I take responsibility.**

Everything premium should help people and organisations live up to that
sentence at greater scale. Nothing premium should make the sentence belong more
to those who can pay.
